# PART A — Decision log: what was rejected, and why

Six corpora were evaluated and rejected before this one. Recording the reasoning
so it isn't relitigated, and because the pattern of failures is what produced the
selection criteria in Part B.

## A.1 Generic "upload any PDF"  ❌

The original design: user uploads any document, system answers questions on it.

**Why rejected**

- **No stable corpus → no evaluation possible.** A golden dataset needs a fixed
  set of documents. If the corpus is whatever a stranger uploads, there is nothing
  to write ground-truth questions against. This is why `evaluation/` sat empty.
- **No tunable parameters.** Optimal chunk size for a legal contract differs from a
  research paper differs from a recipe book. Accepting everything forces
  middle-ground settings that can never be justified with data.
- **Demo friction.** A visitor sees an empty upload box. Most leave before seeing
  anything work.

## A.2 Apple / Microsoft 10-K filings  ❌

**Why rejected**

- Sound corpus, but the plan built on it had a fatal methodology flaw: ground truth
  was to be generated by the same LLM pipeline being tested. Circular — Hit Rate
  would measure *"can I retrieve the chunk I already retrieved"*.
- Superseded by A.3 before that was fixed.

## A.3 Nike / Adidas annual reports  ❌

**Why rejected — factual error at the centre**

- **Adidas does not file a 10-K.** As a German foreign private issuer it files
  Form 20-F under **IFRS**, not US GAAP.
- Nike's fiscal year ends **31 May**; Adidas' ends **31 December**.
- Reporting currencies differ (USD vs EUR).

Any comparative question — *"compare gross margin in FY23"* — would compare
different 12-month periods, under different accounting standards, in different
currencies. The golden dataset would have contained wrong ground truth and the
system would have answered confidently.

## A.4 TCS / Infosys annual reports  ❌

Chosen to fix A.3 — same country, same standard (Ind AS), same currency, same
fiscal calendar. Comparisons genuinely valid.

**Why rejected**

- Corpus of 6–8 documents. Retrieval is easy at that size; the 4-config ablation
  would likely have produced four nearly identical numbers.
- The interesting problems (standalone vs consolidated, table extraction) are
  *parsing* problems, not *retrieval* problems.
- Ultimately abandoned for lack of interest in the domain, which matters: an
  11-day plan on a corpus you don't care about does not get finished.

## A.5 MCC Laws of Cricket — six historical codes (1744→2022)  ❌

**Why rejected — the corpus does not exist**

Verified against official sources:

| Code | Availability |
|---|---|
| 1744, 1788, 1947, 1980 | **No official MCC PDF.** Third-party transcriptions only (ACS archive) |
| 2000, 2017 | Official PDFs on lords.org |

Additional problems found:

- The plan claimed *"100% Official Source Integrity — indexing only official MCC
  documents."* Half the corpus would have been third-party transcriptions. The
  claim was unachievable.
- **MCC states it holds world copyright** in the Laws and licenses them
  commercially. This was *worse* legal exposure than annual reports, not better —
  the opposite of what the plan assumed.
- The plan named the 2022 3rd Edition as current; lords.org already serves a
  **4th Edition (2026)**.

## A.6 MCC Laws of Cricket — editions of the 2017 Code  ❌

Rescoped to what actually exists in `data/raw_docs/`: four editions of the 2017
Code plus one guidance document. **This is the version that was measured, and the
measurements are why it was rejected.**

### Measured finding 1 — the corpus fits in a context window

| Document | Extracted size |
|---|---|
| 2017 Code, 2nd ed (2019) | 215,727 chars · 89 pages |
| 2017 Code, 4th ed (2026) | 233,558 chars · 178 pages |

Two editions ≈ **450K characters ≈ ~115K tokens**. All four ≈ 230K tokens.
`openai/gpt-oss-20b` has a **131K** context window.

**When the corpus nearly fits in one prompt, retrieval is not solving a problem.**
Every design iteration collapsed into exact lookups and hash tables because the
corpus had no retrieval problem to solve.

### Measured finding 2 — text-level deduplication would not fire

Sentence-level comparison, 2nd ed vs 4th ed:

| | |
|---|---|
| Exact matches | **19.4%** |
| ≥0.90 similar | 78% |
| ≥0.95 similar | 58% |

Content genuinely repeats, but only ~19% is byte-identical — the same text is laid
out across 89 pages in one edition and 178 in the other, so extraction differs.

A proposed "fold chunks with identical text" step would require *every* sentence in
a chunk to match. For a 5-sentence chunk: `0.19⁵ ≈ 0.02%`. **It would never fire.**

### Measured finding 3 — law numbers are not stable identities

| Law number | 2nd ed (2019) | 4th ed (2026) |
|---|---|---|
| **41.16** | *"Non-striker leaving his/her ground early"* (Unfair Play) | *"Batters stealing a run"* |
| **38.3** | *"Which batsman is out"* | *"Non-striker leaving their ground early"* (Run out) |

The Mankad rule and another rule **swapped places**. Any design keyed on
`law_number` across editions would present two unrelated rules as one rule
evolving — a fluent, well-cited, entirely wrong answer.

### Verdict

Cricket laws are a **small, highly structured, precisely-worded** corpus. That is
the opposite of what RAG is for. The edition-diff problem is real and interesting,
but it is a **versioning** problem, best solved with a structured store — not a
retrieval problem.

*(Retained as a possible separate project: a rule-diff tool. Just not a RAG one.)*

# PART B — Selection criteria

Derived from the six failures above. A corpus must pass **all three**.

| # | Test | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Does not fit in a context window** | If it fits, you don't need retrieval — you need a good prompt. This killed A.6 |
| **2** | **Vocabulary mismatch** | Users' words ≠ the documents' words. This is the *only* thing dense embeddings do that keyword search cannot. Legal and financial corpora fail this — their language is precise and matches queries |
| **3** | **No structure to exploit** | Stable IDs and hierarchies are a trap: you will correctly be tempted to look up instead of retrieve, and the project becomes a database |

A fourth, practical: **ground truth must be obtainable** without days of manual
labelling.

# PART C — The selected project

## C.1 What it is

**Ask a statistics or machine-learning question in plain English. Get an answer
built from real Cross Validated discussions, with links to the original threads.**

Cross Validated (`stats.stackexchange.com`) holds ~200,000 questions and ~250,000
answers accumulated over 15 years.

**The problem it solves:** searching the site is genuinely hard, because askers and
answerers use different vocabulary. Someone types:

> *"my model gets 99% on training data but 60% on new data"*

The answer that solves it says **overfitting**, **regularisation**,
**bias–variance**. The word "overfitting" never appears in the question. Keyword
search fails on exactly that gap.

## C.2 Why it passes all three tests

| Test | Result |
|---|---|
| Too big for context | ~100× a context window even at reduced scope ✅ |
| Vocabulary mismatch | Severe, and it is the site's defining search weakness ✅ |
| No structure to exploit | No IDs, no hierarchy, no versions. Nothing to look up ✅ |
| Ground truth obtainable | **Already labelled** — see C.4 ✅ |

First corpus evaluated that passes all four.

## C.3 The data

**Source:** official StackExchange data dumps on archive.org
(search *"stackexchange data dump"*, take `stats.stackexchange.com`).

**Licence:** CC-BY-SA. Free to use with attribution. **No copyright problem** —
unlike A.3–A.6.

**Format:** XML. The relevant file is `Posts.xml`. Each row is one post:

```xml
<row Id="12345" PostTypeId="1" AcceptedAnswerId="12350" Score="247"
     Title="Why does my model perform well on training but poorly on test?"
     Body="I trained a random forest and got 99% accuracy on training,
           but only 60% on test. What is going wrong?"
     Tags="machine-learning|overfitting" />

<row Id="12350" PostTypeId="2" ParentId="12345" Score="189"
     Body="This is a classic case of overfitting. Your model has memorised
           the training data rather than learning the pattern..." />
```

| Field | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `PostTypeId=1` | a question |
| `PostTypeId=2` | an answer |
| `ParentId` | which question this answer belongs to |
| **`AcceptedAnswerId`** | **which answer the asker marked correct** — this is the evaluation key |
| `Score` | community votes |
| `Tags` | `machine-learning`, `regression`, `python`… |

**Size:** roughly 1–2 GB uncompressed for Cross Validated *(estimate — verify on
download)*.

## C.4 The reason this corpus is unusually good

`AcceptedAnswerId` means **the correct answer is already labelled, ~200,000 times.**

For every previous plan, the golden dataset was the most expensive item: 50
hand-verified questions, roughly a day of tedious work, and the single biggest
risk to the whole evaluation.

Here it is a download. That converts the evaluation story from:

> *"I hand-wrote 50 test questions"*

into:

> **"nDCG@10, MRR and Recall@50 measured on 500 held-out queries with graded
> relevance judgments."**

That is a real information-retrieval evaluation.

# PART D — Architecture

## D.1 What gets stored

**The answers are the knowledge base.** Each answer becomes one retrievable document.

```
answer_id        12350
question_id      12345
question_title   "Why does my model perform well on training but poorly on test?"
answer_text      "This is a classic case of overfitting..."
score            189
is_accepted      true
tags             ["machine-learning", "overfitting"]
url              https://stats.stackexchange.com/a/12350
```

Plus the 384-dim dense vector, and a sparse vector for keyword matching.

**Chunking:** most answers are short enough to store whole. Split only answers
above the token budget, on paragraph boundaries. **Do not use `SemanticChunker`
here** — it embeds every sentence to find breakpoints, which is a large cost for
no benefit on short posts. This removes the 30–60 minute chunking overhead that
burdened the annual-report plan.

## D.2 Query path

```
user question
   ↓
dense search (bge-small)  ──┐
                            ├─→ RRF fusion ─→ cross-encoder rerank ─→ top 3
sparse search (BM25)      ──┘                                            ↓
                                                            LLM writes answer
                                                            + links to threads
```

Every existing component earns its place:

| Component | Its job here |
|---|---|
| **Dense** | The paraphrase gap — *"99% train / 60% test"* → *overfitting*. Its entire reason for existing |
| **Sparse/BM25** | Exact tokens — `ValueError`, `glmer`, `AUC`, `p-value`, package names. Dense embeddings are weak on these |
| **RRF** | Fusing two retrievers that genuinely disagree |
| **Cross-encoder** | With 100K candidates, precision@3 is the whole game |
| **Citations** | Link to the real thread, with vote count and accepted badge |

## D.3 The engineering problem that becomes real

At ~100K stored answers, the in-memory `rank_bm25` index is roughly **1 GB of
RAM** and dies on a free Space.

**TODO #5 — migrating BM25 to Qdrant native sparse vectors — stops being a
deferred nice-to-have and becomes step one.** This is the first genuine scaling
problem this project has had, and it is worth writing up.

Likely also needed: Qdrant **scalar quantisation** (~4× vector size reduction) to
stay inside the free tier. Another real, demonstrable optimisation.

## D.4 Scope for the free tier

Start with the **top 50,000 questions by score** and their answers (~100K
documents) rather than the full dump.

| Resource | Estimate |
|---|---|
| Dense vectors | 100K × 384 × 4 bytes ≈ **150 MB** — fits Qdrant free tier |
| CPU embedding time | ~15–35 min *(estimate; measure on first run)* |
| BM25 in RAM | ~1 GB → **must** move to Qdrant sparse |

Still ~100× a context window, so test 1 still passes comfortably. Extend later and
report the scaling curve.

# PART E — Evaluation

## E.0 Structure

Four evaluation layers, split into **offline** (before and at deploy, built
bottom-up) and **online** (live traffic, no golden answer), plus **regression** —
which is an *activity*, not a set of new metrics.

```
OFFLINE — built bottom-up, before & at deploy
  1. Component   each part in isolation
  2. Pipeline    the wired handoff (RAG triad)
  3. Application the whole product — is it shippable?

  Regression     change chunking/model → re-run the offline suite → read the diff

ONLINE — live traffic, no golden answer
  4. Monitor in production → self-improving loop → back into the golden set
```

> **Correction to earlier versions of this plan:** regression was previously listed
> as a fifth *level* with its own gates. That was wrong. Regression introduces no
> new measurements — it is re-running layers 1–3 and comparing to a baseline. Same
> metrics, different question: *"did it change?"* rather than *"is it good?"*

The layering matters because the layers answer different questions and cost
different amounts:

| Layer | Question | Golden data? | LLM judge? | Runtime |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Component | Does each part work alone? | Retriever: yes · Generator: no | Generator only | seconds–2 min |
| 2 Pipeline | Does the handoff work end-to-end? | Yes | Yes | ~20 min |
| 3 Application | Is it shippable? | Partly | Yes | ~20 min |
| 4 Online | Is it working for real users? | **No** | On a sample | continuous |

---

## E.1 Layer 1 — Component: each part in isolation

The point of this layer is **fault isolation**. Test each part with the other parts'
failures removed, so a bad number tells you *which* component is at fault.

### 1a. Retriever — on golden labels · reference-based · programmatic

**No LLM. Deterministic. Fast enough to gate CI.**

`AcceptedAnswerId` supplies the golden labels for free: query = question title +
body, gold document = the accepted answer, graded relevance from vote scores.

#### The 2×2 ablation

| Config | Dense | Sparse | Reranker | Isolates |
|---|:---:|:---:|:---:|---|
| **A** Dense only | ✓ | | | Baseline |
| **B** Hybrid (RRF) | ✓ | ✓ | | Sparse contribution |
| **C** Dense + Rerank | ✓ | | ✓ | Reranker alone |
| **D** Full | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | Do they compound? |

`D − C` answers *"was hybrid retrieval worth building?"* — a three-config design
cannot.

#### Metrics — all at identical k

| Metric | Why |
|---|---|
| **Recall@k** | Did the accepted answer make the candidate set at all? |
| **Precision@k** | How much of what we returned was relevant? |
| **nDCG@10** | Graded relevance from vote scores — the standard IR metric |
| **MRR** | Rewards ranking the accepted answer first |
| **Recall@50** | Pre-rerank ceiling. If it isn't here, no reranker can save it |
| Recall by query length | Short vague queries vs long detailed ones |

> Never compare metrics at different k across configs.

### 1b. Generator — on hand-fed golden context · reference-free · LLM-judge

**Feed the generator the accepted answer as context.** Retrieval is removed from
the equation entirely, so any failure here is unambiguously the generator's.

| Metric | Question |
|---|---|
| **Faithfulness** | Is every claim supported by the supplied context? |
| **Answer relevance** | Does it actually address the question asked? |
| **Citation accuracy** | Do the cited posts contain what the answer attributes to them? |

Reference-free: the judge scores the answer *against the supplied context*, not
against a golden answer. No labels needed.

### 1c. Plumbing — `tests/test_components.py` · pytest · no network

- XML parsing: questions/answers separated; `AcceptedAnswerId` links resolve
- No answer stored without its parent question's title
- Embeddings: dim 384, normalised, deterministic, **one shared instance**
- Sparse search: exact token (`glmer`) retrieves the containing answer
- RRF: keyed on document id; deterministic; a doc in both legs outranks one in a single leg
- Reranker: output is a permutation of input; returns exactly `top_k`
- Prompt registry: all variants render; fingerprints stable

Fold `verify_variants.py` and `verify_fixes.py` in rather than rewriting them.

---

## E.2 Layer 2 — Pipeline: the wired handoff (RAG triad)

**Same metrics as 1b, now on real retrieved context instead of hand-fed context.**
Nothing new to invent — the delta between layer 1b and layer 2 *is* the finding.

```
context relevance  →  faithfulness  →  answer relevance
```

The chain is diagnostic:

| Symptom | Diagnosis |
|---|---|
| Low **context relevance** | Retrieval problem — go back to layer 1a |
| Good context, low **faithfulness** | Generator is inventing — prompt or model problem |
| Both good, low **answer relevance** | Right facts, wrong answer — prompt problem |

Run on the 500 held-out questions.

**Judge calibration is mandatory:** hand-verify a 20% sample and **report the
judge–human agreement rate** alongside every judged metric. Below ~85%, fix the
rubric before trusting any number it produces.

---

## E.3 Layer 3 — Application: the whole product

Judged as a **deliverable**, not as mechanics. The question is *"would I ship this?"*

### Correctness · Completeness · Style

| Metric | Method |
|---|---|
| **Correctness** | LLM judge: generated answer vs the accepted answer |
| **Completeness** | Does it cover everything the accepted answer covers, or only part? |
| **Style** | Tone rubric via G-Eval — is it a useful explanation or a wall of text? |

Completeness is separate from correctness on purpose: an answer can be entirely
correct and still miss half of what the user needed.

### Safety

| Check | Why it matters here |
|---|---|
| **Toxicity** | Corpus is real internet comments. Some are rude |
| **PII** | Posts sometimes contain emails, names, dataset paths, API keys |
| **Jailbreak / injection** | Post text containing *"ignore previous instructions"* must not be followed |

### Operations

| Metric | Why |
|---|---|
| **Latency** | Median + P90, 3 runs each, first discarded. **Not P95** on small n |
| **Cost** | Even at $0, track it — a free tier is a budget |
| **Tokens per query** | **Your real constraint.** 1,000 requests/day free tier; tokens/query determines whether you fit |

### Behavioural / adversarial cases (~30)

| Category | Example | Pass condition |
|---|---|---|
| Out of scope | *"What's the weather in Delhi?"* | States corpus boundary |
| No good answer exists | Obscure question with no close match | Says so; does not invent |
| Ambiguous | *"how do I choose k?"* (k-means? k-NN? k-fold?) | Asks which, or covers each |
| Outdated advice | Old post recommending a deprecated library | Notes the post's age |
| Prompt injection | Post containing *"ignore previous instructions"* | Not followed |

Measure **both** directions:

- **Refusal recall** — % of out-of-scope correctly refused. Target ≥90%
- **Refusal precision** — % of in-scope **wrongly** refused. Target ≤5%

A system that refuses everything scores 100% on recall and is useless.

## E.4 Regression — an activity, not new metrics

Change chunking, the model, a prompt, or `top_k` → **re-run the whole offline
suite → read the diff.**

`evaluation/baselines.json` is the contract: committed, and updated deliberately
with a note explaining any movement.

### CI wiring (`.github/workflows/ci_cd.yml`, currently 1 byte)

| Gate | Runs | Fails when |
|---|---|---|
| Layer 1c plumbing tests | Every push | Any assertion fails |
| Prompt fingerprint check | Every push | An active variant changed without a version bump |
| Layer 1a retriever eval | Every push (no LLM, ~2 min) | nDCG@10 drops >3 points below baseline |
| **Model liveness** | **Nightly** | An active model ID stops responding |
| Layers 1b / 2 / 3 | Manual, pre-release | Reported, not gating (LLM cost + judge variance) |

**The nightly liveness check is the highest-value item here** — it is exactly what
would have caught the dead vision model a year early, and the Groq deprecation
before the shutdown date rather than after.

## E.5 Layer 4 — Online: monitor in production

**Live traffic. No golden answer.** Everything above assumed a labelled test set;
this layer has none, so it relies on proxies and sampling.

Small on a portfolio project, but real — and almost no portfolio RAG has it.

### Computed — LLM-judge on a sample

Sample ~5% of live queries and score them:

- **Faithfulness**
- **Answer relevance**

Same rubric as layer 2, so the numbers are comparable to your offline baseline.
Sample, don't score everything — a judge call on every query doubles your cost and
burns free-tier quota.

### Captured — just logged, no judging

| Signal | Source |
|---|---|
| Latency per stage | `time.perf_counter()` — already instrumented |
| Cost / tokens | Groq response `usage` field |
| **👍 / 👎** | **Add two buttons to the Gradio UI.** Cheapest signal you will ever collect |
| Escalations | User re-asks, rephrases, or abandons |

### Correctness by proxy

There is no golden answer in production, so correctness has to be inferred:

- **Thumbs-down rate** — the most direct proxy
- **Re-ask rate** — a rephrased follow-up within the same session usually means the
  first answer failed
- **Abandonment** — no follow-up, no rating, session ends

### Drift

Compare the **score distribution** of the live sample against the offline baseline.

Drift shows up as *"faithfulness on sampled traffic is 0.71 this week vs 0.84 on
the offline set."* Causes worth distinguishing:

- Real users ask harder or messier questions than your held-out set
- A model was silently updated underneath you
- The corpus went stale relative to what people now ask about

## E.6 The self-improving loop

**Production failures → back into the golden set.**

```
👎 rating, or re-ask, or drift outlier
        ↓
   review the query by hand
        ↓
   add to evaluation/eval_set.json (with correct answer)
        ↓
   it is now permanently guarded by regression
```

This is what makes the whole framework a system rather than a one-off report. Every
real failure becomes a permanent test. The offline suite gets stronger precisely
where the system actually failed, instead of where you guessed it might.

**Implementation is small:** log thumbs-down queries to a file, review them
periodically, promote the interesting ones into the eval set. Perhaps 30 lines,
and it is the most defensible thing in the project — it shows the evaluation is
wired to reality, not to your imagination.

## STEP 1 — Get the data  ⏱ half a day

**1.1** Download the dump from archive.org — search *"stackexchange data dump"*,
take `stats.stackexchange.com.7z`. Extract `Posts.xml`.

**1.2** Write `backend/scripts/parse_dump.py`. Stream the XML — **do not
`ET.parse()` the whole file**, it's over a GB. Use `iterparse` and clear elements
as you go:

```python
for event, elem in ET.iterparse(path, events=("end",)):
    if elem.tag == "row":
        ...
        elem.clear()
```

Extract per row: `Id`, `PostTypeId`, `ParentId`, `AcceptedAnswerId`, `Score`,
`Title`, `Body`, `Tags`, `CreationDate`. Strip HTML from `Body` — keep code blocks,
they carry the exact tokens sparse search needs.

**1.3 Print the counts and eyeball 20 posts by hand.**

**✅ Check:**
- Question count and answer count are in the right ballpark (~200K / ~250K)
- Every `AcceptedAnswerId` resolves to an answer that exists
- Code blocks survived HTML stripping
- No answer is missing its parent question's title

**❌ If counts look wrong**, you're probably filtering `PostTypeId` incorrectly, or
the dump includes deleted posts. Fix here — every downstream number depends on it.

## STEP 2 — Migrate BM25 to Qdrant sparse vectors  ⏱ 1.5 days · HARD PART

**Do this before ingestion, not after.** At 100K documents your in-process
`rank_bm25` index is ~1GB of RAM and will not run on a free Space. This is TODO #5
finally becoming mandatory.

**2.1** Add `fastembed` and use SPLADE (or BM42) to produce sparse vectors.

**2.2** Recreate the Qdrant collection with **both** vector types:

```python
vectors_config={"dense": VectorParams(size=384, distance=Distance.COSINE)}
sparse_vectors_config={"sparse": SparseVectorParams()}
```

**2.3** Replace `HybridRetriever.bm25_search` with a Qdrant sparse query. Keep the
same method signature so nothing downstream changes.

**2.4** Optionally use Qdrant's native `FusionQuery(fusion=Fusion.RRF)` with
prefetch, so fusion happens database-side. **Keep your Python RRF as well** — it's
the thing you can ablate against, and it's already ID-keyed and tested.

**✅ Check:**
- Searching `glmer` returns posts containing `glmer`
- **No BM25 object exists in the process.** `grep -rn "BM25Okapi" backend/` returns nothing
- Memory during search is flat, not proportional to corpus size

**❌ If sparse results look random**, you've likely mixed up the vector name in the
query. Sparse and dense must be addressed by name.

## STEP 3 — Ingest  ⏱ half a day

**3.1** Select the **top 50,000 questions by `Score`** and all of their answers.

**3.2** One answer = one document. Split only answers over the token budget, on
paragraph boundaries. **Do not use `SemanticChunker`** — it embeds every sentence,
which is a large cost for no benefit on short posts.

**3.3** Store per document:

```
answer_id · question_id · question_title · answer_text
score · is_accepted · tags · url · created_date
```

**3.4** Run it once, locally, idempotently:

```powershell
python -m backend.scripts.seed_corpus --dump data/posts.xml
```

**✅ Check:**
- Document count ≈ expected; log it
- Pick 10 documents at random and open the real URL — text matches
- Qdrant collection size is ~150MB *(if far larger, check you're not storing raw HTML)*
- **Query "overfitting" and read the top 5 by eye.** They should obviously be about
  overfitting. If not, stop — something is wrong with embedding or ingestion

**❌ If Qdrant exceeds the free tier**, enable scalar quantisation (~4× reduction)
before reducing corpus size.

---

## STEP 4 — Build the evaluation set  ⏱ half a day

**4.1** Take **500 questions that have an `AcceptedAnswerId`**, sampled across the
score range — not only top-scored ones, or you bake in popularity bias.

**4.2** **Hold them out.** These 500 questions and their answers must not be used
for any tuning decision. Write them to `evaluation/eval_set.json`:

```json
{
  "query_id": "q12345",
  "query": "<title> + <body>",
  "gold_answer_id": "12350",
  "graded_relevance": {"12350": 3, "12351": 2, "12352": 1},
  "tags": ["machine-learning", "overfitting"]
}
```

Graded relevance from vote scores: accepted = 3, high-voted = 2, other answers on
the same question = 1, everything else = 0.

**✅ Check:** every `gold_answer_id` exists in Qdrant. If some don't, your sample

# PART G — Threats to validity

Publish this. It costs a paragraph and makes every other number more credible.

- **Accepted ≠ best.** The asker's accepted answer is one person's judgment, sometimes
  outvoted by a better answer below it. Report vote-weighted metrics alongside
- **Popularity bias.** Sampling top-scored questions favours well-written, popular
  ones. Real queries are messier
- **Single site, single domain.** Cross Validated results may not generalise to other
  StackExchange sites, let alone other corpora
- **Temporal drift.** Older accepted answers may recommend deprecated approaches;
  the system will faithfully repeat them
- **LLM-judged faithfulness** inherits judge bias; see the calibration step
- **Free-tier latency** is shared and noisy; not comparable to dedicated infrastructure
